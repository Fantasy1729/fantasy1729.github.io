Soundtrack to Survival

How Gen Z Uses Music to Cope With Mental Health

Introduction: Music as Modern Medicine

If you're part of Gen Z, chances are music has done more for your emotional health than you might realize. It’s not just something we play in the background. It’s the thing we turn to at 2AM when we can’t sleep, or when school stress feels like too much, or when we just need to feel *something*.

For our Music 137 final project, we wanted to explore how different kinds of music—lo-fi beats, sad rap, and mood-based playlists—have become central to how Gen Z copes with stress, anxiety, and everything in between. This isn’t just a musical trend; it’s emotional survival through sound.

Lo-fi Beats: The Sound of Stillness

There’s something almost magical about lo-fi. You press play and suddenly the world gets quieter—even when it’s not. Lo-fi music is full of soft beats, jazzy chords, ambient textures, and that soothing vinyl crackle that sounds like a hug for your brain. It’s like the opposite of chaos.

What really stood out to me is how lo-fi isn’t trying to distract you. It’s just there—reliable, consistent, mellow. It helps you think, but also lets you zone out. No lyrics telling you how to feel, just vibes that make space for your own emotions to breathe.

"I usually listen to lofi beats when I study. It allows me to clear my head and focus on whatever work I have to do."

Personally, I’ve come to think of lo-fi as the musical equivalent of deep breathing. You put it on, and your thoughts stop racing so much. Whether you’re journaling or doomscrolling or just trying to calm down before bed, lo-fi offers this sort of invisible structure.

Mood-Based Playlists: Emotional Curation

You know that feeling when your playlist gets you more than your therapist? That’s kind of the whole idea behind mood-based playlists. We’re talking about playlists like “late night drive,” “songs to cry to,” or “anxiety relief.” They’re not organized by genre—they’re built around vibes.

What I found fascinating is how these playlists have become emotional first-aid kits. You’re feeling a certain way, and boom—there’s a playlist for that. You don’t even have to know what’s wrong. Sometimes, the first song hits and you go, *“Oh. Yeah. That’s what I needed.”*

"Sometimes I don't even know what I'm feeling until I hear it in a song."

It’s more than just music. It’s a way of checking in with yourself. Some playlists help you sit with your feelings and let them wash over you. Others help you shift your mood entirely. And the fact that these playlists are so accessible—just a search away on Spotify or YouTube—makes them even more valuable.

Sad Rap: When Music Hits Too Close

Sad rap is like a confessional booth with a beat. Artists like JuiceWRLD, XXXTentacion, Lil Peep, and NF don’t just rap—they vent, reflect, and let their pain spill into the mic. Listening to them feels like reading someone’s diary, except it rhymes.

These songs hit different. They’re filled with lyrics about heartbreak, mental health, substance use, and just feeling *tired*. And because a lot of these artists died young, their music takes on an even deeper weight. It’s haunting—but also healing.

"You saved millions, but no one saved you."

What’s wild is how much the production supports the emotion. You’ll hear minor chords, lo-fi beats, distorted vocals, and long echo effects. It sounds how numbness feels. There’s something strangely comforting about that. Like someone finally put your feelings into a song.

And the fan communities? They’re something else. The comment sections are like group therapy. People drop lines like “this song got me through the worst” or “I feel like someone finally gets me.” It’s music, but it’s also connection.

What Students Say

To see how all of this plays out on campus, we talked to a few students at Duke. Almost everyone we spoke to had a “go-to” playlist for when life gets overwhelming. Whether it was lo-fi while studying, or sad rap after a breakup, people had music rituals built into their coping.

One person told us, “I don’t even need to talk about it. I just throw on this playlist, and it helps me feel like I’m not the only one.” Another said music gave them “space to cry without having to explain anything.”

And honestly? Same.

Conclusion: Music is Emotional Infrastructure

At the end of the day, we’re not just listening to music—we’re leaning on it. We’re using it to get through the tough stuff, to understand our own minds, to breathe. It’s personal, it’s powerful, and it’s absolutely part of how Gen Z is surviving.

If you’ve ever cried to a playlist called “songs that hurt (but heal),” you already know. Music isn’t just a background vibe—it’s our therapy, our mirror, and sometimes, our only safe space.
